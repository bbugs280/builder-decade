Here's a belief that quietly kills more solo products than any technical problem: *"I'll start charging once it's good enough."* The product never gets good enough, so the charge never comes, so the product never gets a real signal about whether it matters. The fix is to flip the order entirely.

**Charge early. Charge small. Let the first $5 be the proof, not the goal.**

## What the first charge actually is

It's not revenue — it's a *measurement*. A stranger willingly handing over money, even a tiny amount, is the single most information-dense signal you can get about a product. It settles three questions at once:

- **Does anyone want this?** A compliment is free. Five dollars is not. Money is the only feedback that costs the giver something, which is why it's the only feedback you can trust.
- **Is the problem real enough to pay for?** People say "cool idea" about things they'd never buy. They pay for things that remove a genuine, felt pain — and the payment is how you find out which one you've built.
- **Does the loop work?** The first charge exercises the whole machine end to end — the page, the price, the payment, the delivery. A product that has never charged is a product with an untested checkout, and an untested checkout is a landmine.

The dollar amount is almost irrelevant. The *existence* of the charge is the milestone.

## Why waiting is the wrong move

The "charge later" plan has a specific, predictable failure mode: **later never comes.** Here's the math on why:

- Every week you don't charge, you're polishing against your own assumption of what's valuable — with zero evidence. You're paying in time to *guess* what you could learn for $0 by just asking the market.
- The product drifts. Without the anchoring signal of "what will people actually pay for," you build features nobody asked for, because you have no signal pointing at what they *would* ask for.
- The stakes rise. The longer you wait, the more "the launch" has to justify the sunk time, so the launch gets heavier, later, and more likely to disappoint.

Charging early is not greedy. It's *de-risking* — the cheapest possible way to find out if you're building the right thing before you build more of it.

## How to price the first thing

The goal of the first price is to be *paid*, not to be *profitable*. So price for conversion, not for value:

- **Charge less than the thing is worth, and say so.** "Launch price, going up later" is honest and it converts. You're not discounting value, you're pricing the *validation*.
- **Pick a number that's too small to think about.** For a consumer tool, that's a coffee — $5. The point isn't the revenue, it's that nobody has to deliberate. You want the *yes* to be automatic.
- **Make paying the shortest path.** One button, one price, one clear thing-you-get. Every field or choice between "want" and "paid" is a place the person walks away.

You can raise the price later — and you should, once the signal says people want it. But the first price's job is to *exist* and to *get paid*, not to be right.

## What the first $5 unlocks

After that first charge, everything changes, and none of it is about the five dollars:

- **You have a user who is not you.** A paying customer asks better questions, reports real bugs, and brings the next person. That's the flywheel starting.
- **You have a number to show.** "People pay for this" is a fact now, not a hope. It's the difference between "I'm building something" and "I have a product."
- **You know which half to build next.** The first payer always tells you, one way or another, what they actually wanted. That's your roadmap, handed to you for five dollars.

This is the revenue half of [the ship-one-tiny-thing loop](/posts/ship-one-tiny-thing/) — ship small, then *charge* small. And it pairs with [how to get your first users](/posts/how-to-get-first-users-solo/): you can't charge until *someone* has seen the thing.
