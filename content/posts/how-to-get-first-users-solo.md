Build the thing, and a certain silence follows. You ship it. You tell a few people. And then — nothing. No users, no feedback, no sign that anyone heard. Most solo builders assume this means the product failed. Usually it means something simpler: **you built it, but you never actually distributed it.**

This is the part nobody writes about, because the people who write about building are good at building, not at the awkward part that comes after. Distribution is not another build skill. It's a social skill — which is exactly why introverts assume they're bad at it. They're not. They've just been handed the wrong playbook.

The standard advice is built for extroverts: cold DM everyone, network relentlessly, work the room, "hustle." For a solo builder who'd rather write than network, that advice is not merely unpleasant — it's *wrong for you*. The introvert playbook is different, and it leans on the exact things introverts already do well: writing carefully, going deep, and showing instead of telling.

## The core shift: stop selling, start showing

Most solo builders think distribution means *telling people about the product*. It doesn't. For an introvert, it means the opposite: **put the product where people can see it, and let it speak for itself.**

A cold DM is a request — "please pay attention to me." A post that *shows the thing working* is a gift — "here's something you might find useful." Introverts are bad at the first and good at the second. So the entire playbook is built around making requests disappear and replacing them with demos.

## Write, don't network

Every minute spent cold-messaging is a minute spent on a channel with a ~2% response rate and a high emotional tax. The introvert equivalent is building in public: **one honest post about how you built the thing reaches more people than a hundred DMs, because it compounds.**

Here's the mechanism most people miss. A DM reaches one person and dies. A public post reaches whoever sees it *today*, but it also lives on — in search results, in someone's saved links, in the archive of a community. Six months later it's still quietly introducing you to people while you sleep. That's leverage you can't get from outreach, and it's why "write, don't network" isn't a consolation prize for shy people — it's the strictly better strategy for anyone patient enough to use it.

The only requirement: the writing has to be *real*. Not "I'm excited to announce." The actual mechanics, the dead ends, the thing that almost made you quit. Introverts write that kind of thing well because they've been thinking about it alone for months.

## Go where buyers already gather

The second move is a matter of location, not effort. Don't try to drag people to your product. **Post where they already are, doing what they already do.**

If you've built a tool, the communities are obvious: r/SideProject, r/selfhosted, a niche forum for the exact problem you solve. You don't pitch there — you show. Post the thing working, answer questions honestly, leave when there's nothing left to say. The rule is: never ask for an install, never ask for upvotes. Just be useful in public and let the product be the proof.

The undersold advantage: most of these spaces are already flooded with marketers, so an actual builder showing a real artifact stands out immediately. The introvert's discomfort with self-promotion reads as *earnestness*, which is the most trusted tone in the room.

## Go deep on one channel

The biggest distribution mistake solo builders make isn't picking the wrong channel — it's picking ten. One shallow post in five places beats nothing, but it loses to deep presence in one place.

Pick **one** channel and commit to it for months. Ten to thirty real posts, not three. The compounding works the same way it does in building: your first post gets ignored, the tenth gets noticed, the thirtieth makes you *known* in that space. When you're known, distribution stops being an activity and becomes a *property* — people come to you.

Depth also does something practical for introverts: it makes the social part *predictable*. One community, whose norms you learn once, whose people recognize you. That's a drastically lower emotional cost than cold-reaching strangers forever.

## The quiet leverage loop

Put the three together and you get a self-reinforcing system:

1. **Build in public** — write about the real thing as you build it.
2. **Show up where buyers are** — post artifacts, not pitches.
3. **Go deep on the one channel** that works, and ignore the rest.

None of this requires being outgoing. It requires being *consistent*, which introverts do better than anyone, and *patient*, which is the one distribution skill money can't buy.

The brutal truth underneath it all: **most solo projects don't die from a bad product — they die from zero distribution.** The person who ships one thing and then spends the next three months quietly earning ten loyal users in one community beats the person who ships five things into the void. Distribution is not a talent you either have or don't. It's a system, and it runs on the talents introverts already own.
