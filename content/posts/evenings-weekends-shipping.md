The advice is always to "go all in." Quit the job, burn the boats, commit fully. But most people can't — and most people who *do* still need the bridge that comes before the leap. So here's the less romantic, far more useful system: **ship on evenings and weekends, in the margins, and let the calendar do the heavy lifting.**

## The honest calendar math

A side project doesn't die from a lack of passion. It dies from a lack of *time to show up consistently*. So the first move is brutal and clarifying: **count the actual hours.**

- A full day job plus a commute leaves most people with 1–2 *available* hours on a weekday, and 4–8 on each weekend day. That's not "a few." That's **10–15 real hours a week** — which is more than enough, but only if you *guard* it.
- The enemy isn't a small number of hours. It's *unclaimed* hours that leak into scrolling, chores, and "I'll start tomorrow." A calendar slot that isn't named is a slot that disappears.

So: pick your hours ahead of time, write them down, and treat them like a meeting you can't cancel. Two weekday evenings + one weekend morning is 8–12 protected hours — a serious project, run on lunch money of time.

## Energy before tactics

The hours don't help if you're tired when you arrive at them. So the system optimizes energy *first*, because a focused hour beats three exhausted ones:

- **Put the hard thing first in the session.** The moment you sit down is the moment your brain is freshest. Do the thinking task then, not the easy admin that'll still be easy at midnight.
- **Ship before you polish, always.** "Done" costs less energy than "beautiful," and it keeps the [ship one tiny thing](/posts/ship-one-tiny-thing/) loop turning. Perfection is an energy tax on a part-time builder you genuinely can't afford.
- **Protect the streak, not the output.** A two-hour session that ships something tiny beats a heroic weekend that burns you out for a month. Consistency compounds; intensity doesn't.

## The one rule that keeps it alive

Systems fail in a specific way: they start with a burst, then a week gets skipped, then "I stopped doing it." The countermeasure is a rule so small you can't break it:

**Never let the gap be two weeks.**

One bad week is normal — life happens. But the moment you miss *two* consecutive weeks, the project is statistically close to dead, because the momentum is gone and the guilt of restarting is heavier than the cost of a single session. So the rule isn't "never miss." It's "never miss twice in a row." That's a rule you can actually keep, and it's the difference between a side project that runs for *years* and one that runs for a *month*.

## Why the margins are enough

The counterintuitive truth about part-time building is that the constraint is a feature. A part-time builder can't over-build, can't gold-plate, can't chase every shiny feature — because there aren't enough hours. The same budget that looks like a disadvantage is quietly enforcing the discipline (ship small, ship often, stop when it works) that full-time builders have to fake with willpower.

Your evenings and weekends aren't the *start* of your builder career — they're the training ground where the muscle actually forms. [The beginner's on-ramp](/posts/ai-beginner-start/) will tell you where to start; this system is how you keep *going* once you've started. And it's the time-budget that makes a [one-person company](/posts/one-person-team/) possible before it becomes your *only* company.
