I have called myself a solo founder, a solo builder, and — on bad days — "just me." Each word was accurate. Each one also did work I hadn't asked it to do.

Nobody hands you a title when you start. You pick one, usually in the first week, usually in a bio field with a character limit. Then you spend years living inside it.

So it's worth ten minutes of care.

## The words are not synonyms

**Solo founder** describes a structural fact. A company exists, there is equity, and you are the one who started it. It says nothing about headcount, revenue, or ambition. Founder is a role in a cap table.

**Solopreneur** describes an operating mode. One person does the building, the selling, and the books. It implies a business that stays one person, deliberately. Entrepreneur carries the ambition; solo carries the constraint.

**Solo builder** is the one I reach for most, and it's the least loaded. It claims the work, not the legal structure. Useful when the company is real but the story is still about the making.

That distinction matters because the two big words point in opposite directions:

- *Founder* points at the entity — "this could grow, hire, raise."
- *Solopreneur* points at the operator — "this is designed to stay small and profitable."

Pick the words at random and your bio quietly promises something you never decided. That's the whole risk.

## Who is actually reading

The reception differs far more than the words do.

**Investors and accelerators** read "solopreneur" as a stated ceiling. That can be exactly what you want — it filters out a conversation you don't want — but if you use it while actually raising, you are arguing against yourself in your own first line.

**Corporate hiring managers** — the ones who might hire you back — read "founder" as risk and "solopreneur" as flight risk. Neither word helps there. "Builder" travels better, because it names a skill rather than a departure.

**Customers** mostly don't care, with one exception: B2B buyers want to know if you'll exist next year. "Solopreneur" invites a bus-factor question. Have an answer ready, because it's a fair question.

**Other builders** are the audience that reads most closely and buys least. They will not care what you call yourself. They'll care what you shipped.

## The test I use now

One question, asked honestly:

> **Do I want this to stay one person, or is one person the current state?**

If one person is the *goal*, say solopreneur. It's not a consolation prize — a deliberately small, profitable, single-operator business is a legitimate design, and naming it prevents years of accidental scope creep toward a company you never wanted.

If one person is the *current state*, say founder or builder, and don't over-commit either way. You haven't decided yet. Saying so is fine. Saying "solopreneur" when you mean "not yet" is the one choice that costs you later, because you will have to walk it back in front of the people who read it first.

## Three practical rules

**1. Pick one word and use it everywhere.** Bio, domain, email signature, LinkedIn headline. Mixed vocabulary reads as uncertainty, and uncertainty is the only thing readers reliably punish.

**2. Let the work carry the ambition.** If you're building something with real scope, the writing will show it. You don't need "founder" to do that labour, and using the grander word while shipping small invites exactly the comparison you don't want.

**3. Change it when the fact changes — and say why.** A rename with a stated reason reads as maturity. A rename that happens silently reads as drift. One line is enough: "I called myself a solo founder for two years. I've hired someone. The word is now founder."

## What I actually landed on

I use **solo builder** in writing about the work, because it's true and it commits me to nothing I haven't decided. I use **founder** in the places where a legal entity is the relevant fact. I've stopped using solopreneur entirely — not because it's lesser, but because I never actually chose the constraint it implies; I'm just currently a team of one and I don't want to promise either direction yet.

The word was never the achievement. But it is the first thing anyone reads, and it sets what they expect next.

Choose it on purpose, then go build the thing that makes it true.
