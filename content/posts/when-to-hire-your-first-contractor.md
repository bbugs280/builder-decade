# When to Hire Your First Contractor (and When Not To)

Every blog that tells you to "hire your first contractor" is written by someone who profits when you do. The staffing agencies, the fractional-CXO marketplaces, the "done-for-you" studios — they all want the same transaction, and their advice bends toward it.

That's the first thing to understand: **hire advice has a sales team behind it.** So here's the receipts-first version, built from the actual numbers solo founders report, not from a vendor's funnel.

## The Real Question Isn't "Who" — It's "When"

Solo founders don't hire because the work runs out. They hire because the *founder* runs out — of hours, of focus, or of the specific skill the next step needs.

The timing data is clearer than most people expect. Carta's data on solo founders shows the median solo founder makes their **first hire at 399 days** after founding — about **13 months**. (Multi-founder teams, for comparison, wait even longer: 480 days.)

Read that carefully: the *median* solo founder runs for over a year before spending a single dollar on a team. That's not stinginess. It's the structural reality of the solo game — you stay lean until the lean machine itself becomes the bottleneck.

The mistake isn't hiring too early or too late in a vacuum. It's hiring the wrong *shape* of help for the stage you're actually in.

## What a First Hire Actually Costs

The reason 399 days is a sensible default is that a *first employee* is expensive in a way a *first contractor* is not. Run the numbers side by side:

- **Full-time hire:** a $90K salary plus ~$25K benefits plus equity works out to roughly **$145K in year-one cost** — before you count the 3–6 months to hire and onboard, and the emotional + financial cost of a bad fit you now have to unwind.
- **Senior contractor:** a $125/hr specialist at 20 hours a week for 48 weeks is about **$120K** — but you can scale up or down monthly and, crucially, exit with 30 days' notice if it isn't working.

The quality gap cuts *toward* the contractor early on. A $125/hr contractor usually has 10+ years of experience; a $90K full-time hire often has 3–5. At the stage where you need someone who operates independently with no training, experience is the entire difference between "figured it out" and "needs to be taught."

If you're going to hand over equity, know the market price: **Carta's data on 8,000+ first-ten-hire grants shows the first employee's median grant is 1.49%** of fully diluted shares over four years. That's real ownership — make sure the role earns it rather than just filling a chair.

## Contractor vs. Employee — the Actual Trade

The clean way to think about it:

- **A contractor ghosts you for one project. A bad employee costs you a year of payroll.** Both are recoverable, but one is asymmetric.
- **Contractors are for defined scope; employees are for ownership.** If the work has a clear end (a landing page, a brand, a 3-month mobile build), contract it. If the work is ongoing and *central* to the product (core engineering, the thing you can't afford to be one degree removed from), that's where full-time eventually earns its premium.

The roles that skew contractor-first early on, in practice, are the ones with **measurable output or bounded scope**: performance marketing (until ad spend is big enough to justify it), content and SEO at a senior-but-part-time level, project-based design, specialized engineering for a specific build, and finance/bookkeeping until you're post-Series-A. The roles that *resist* contracting are the ones where judgment compounds daily and you can't spec it in advance — your core product, your key accounts.

## The Two Mistakes That Actually Sink Solo Founders

There are two failure modes, and they're mirror images:

1. **Hiring by anxiety.** The queue is intimidating, so you throw money at it to feel like you're making progress. You hire a "strategist" before you've validated a channel, and now you're paying someone to guess. A strategist tells you *what* to do; a specialist scales what you've already proven *works*. Validate first, hire to scale second.

2. **Not hiring by identity.** You call yourself a "solo founder" like it's a badge you can't remove, so you hold onto tasks that stopped being worth your hour. At some point the highest-leverage thing you can buy isn't a tool — it's an hour of someone else's shoulder on a problem you've been carrying alone for six months.

Both mistakes come from the same place: treating hiring as an identity decision ("am I still a *solo* founder?") instead of a math decision ("is my hour worth more building, or unblocking?").

## A Simple Rule That Holds

**Contract the commodity; keep the compounding.**

If the task has a spec and a finish line, it's commodity — contract it and move on. If the task *is* the product, or the judgment that builds the moat, keep it in-house even when it's slow. The solo advantage isn't that you do everything yourself. It's that you know exactly which things are worth doing yourself, and everything else is negotiable.

Your first hire should feel boring. Not a validator of your ambition — a lever you pull because the math says the machine is finally the limiting factor.
