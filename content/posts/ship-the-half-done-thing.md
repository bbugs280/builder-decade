The instinct is to wait. Finish it. Polish it. Make it *good* before anyone sees it. The problem is that "good" is a moving target you can't hit without outside eyes — so you polish in private, forever, and the thing never ships.

The fix is uncomfortable and it works: **ship the half-done thing, on purpose, and let real feedback do the editing you can't do alone.**

## Why "done in private" is a trap

Polishing in private has two hidden costs that usually don't show up on any timeline:

- **You're editing against yourself.** The only person judging the work is the person closest to it — who is exactly the wrong person. You can't see the parts that confuse a stranger, because you wrote them. You can't hear the missing step, because you already know it.
- **The novelty decay clock is running.** The longer a thing sits unfinished, the less you want to finish it. That's the week-three wall in its purest form: the energy to ship doesn't survive the distance you insist on keeping between "started" and "seen."

"Good enough to show" is a real milestone, and it arrives a *lot* sooner than "done." Treat arriving there as the ship point, not the halfway point.

## What feedback actually buys you

When you show a half-done thing, the feedback you get back is not a grade — it's a *map*. Three specific things it tells you that you cannot learn alone:

1. **Which direction is wrong.** A stranger spots a dead end in ten seconds that you'd defend for a month, because they don't have your sunk cost.
2. **What's actually confusing.** The step you thought was obvious turns out to be the single thing nobody follows. You'd never find it yourself — it's invisible to you.
3. **Whether anyone wants it at all.** The most valuable feedback is silence followed by a flat question: "what's it for?" That's the cheapest market test there is, and it only costs a half-done draft.

Each of these is a defection risk uncovered before you've sunk the full cost. That's the whole economic case for shipping early: **feedback is cheaper when the thing is still cheap to change.**

## The exact move

- **Pick the smallest shippable slice.** Not the feature, the *slice* — the one thing a person could look at and react to. A landing page. A single screen. A paragraph that makes the argument.
- **Show one real person, not the void.** Broadcasting into a crowd is performance; showing one person who'll actually respond is *editing*. One honest reader beats a hundred silent impressions.
- **Ask one specific question.** Not "what do you think?" — that invites politeness. Ask "what's confusing here?" or "would you pay for this?" A pointed question gets a pointed answer.
- **Ship the revision, not the debate.** Take the one piece of feedback that matters, fold it in, ship again. The loop *is* the product.

## Why this beats polishing

Here's the honest part: the half-done thing you ship will be worse than the finished thing you *imagine*. That's fine, because the imagined finished thing has a flaw — it's imaginary. The real thing, shipped and revised, exists, and existence is what compounding runs on (see the [ship one tiny thing](/posts/ship-one-tiny-thing/) loop — this is that system applied to *one* launch instead of a weekly cadence).

Perfectionism isn't a quality standard. It's a delay mechanism dressed up as one. The builders who ship early and revise in public aren't sloppier than you — they're running a tighter feedback loop, and it's the loop that wins.
