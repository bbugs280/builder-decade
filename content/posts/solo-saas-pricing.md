Ask a solo founder how they set their price, and most will admit they didn't. They picked a number that felt defensible, rounded it to something clean, and moved on to the part they actually enjoy. Pricing is the chore nobody wants to do — and it's the single highest-leverage dial a one-person company has.

Here's the thing the "raise your prices" advice never explains: for a solo founder, **the number matters less than the model.** Whether you charge $9 or $29 a month is a rounding decision. Whether you charge *recurring* at all — versus a one-time license, versus per-seat, versus usage — is a strategic one. And the data is finally clear on which direction wins.

## The number is a guess. The model is a decision.

Stripe Atlas analyzed thousands of solo-founded startups and found that the top 10% of solo founders don't price by a more clever number — they're **26 percentage points more likely (B2B) and 20 points more likely (B2C) to bill on a recurring basis** than their middle-decile peers.

Think about what that actually means. It's not that recurring billing *makes* you grow. It's that recurring billing is a proxy for a deeper choice: **selling an outcome someone keeps paying for, instead of a product someone buys once.** A one-time purchase means you re-earn every dollar from scratch, forever. A recurring relationship means the dollar you earned last month is still there this month — and compounding on top of it.

For a solo founder, that distinction is the difference between a treadmill and an asset. The median solo B2B founder makes **four times the revenue** of the median solo B2C founder by month 24. That's not a small edge. That's an entire business model outrunning another one with the same hours.

## Cost-plus is a spec sheet. Value is the hours they don't lose.

The default way to price — "take your costs, add a margin" — has a fatal flaw for a solo builder: it anchors the price to *your* inputs, not the buyer's outcome. Your server costs $20 a month, your time costs a few hours, so you charge $49 and feel fine.

But the buyer isn't buying your server. **They're buying the hours they don't lose** — the thing that used to take their team three days and now takes one click. If your tool saves a company $4,000 a month in labor, charging $49 for it isn't humble, it's *misreading the size of the gift*. Cost-plus turns a moat into a commodity because it invites the comparison you least want: "could I build this myself for less?" When the price is tied to what it costs *you*, the buyer starts doing build-vs-buy math. When the price is tied to what it saves *them*, that math stops mattering.

The villain here isn't a competitor. It's **underpricing yourself into the treadmill** — signing up for a thousand customers when a hundred at ten times the price would have built the same revenue with a tenth of the support load.

## The decision rule, in order

Pricing a solo SaaS isn't one question. It's three, in strict order:

1. **Recurring, or one-time?** Default to recurring unless the product is genuinely a one-shot utility (a converter, a single export). Recurring is the top-decile habit for a reason — it's the only model where yesterday's work still earns tomorrow. If your product resists a subscription, that's a signal about the product, not just the price.

2. **Value, not cost.** Replace "what did it cost me to run?" with "what would it cost them to not have this?" — the hours, the errors, the rework, the missed revenue. Charge a price that's a *fraction* of that, not a multiple of your costs. If you can't articulate the buyer's lost-hours number, you don't yet know what you're selling.

3. **B2B over B2C when you can.** The gap is stark — median solo B2B revenue runs roughly four times B2C. Businesses anchor to ROI and pay monthly without flinching once the value is clear. Consumers anchor to "is this worth $9?" — a brutal comparison for a solo builder without a marketing budget.

## The uncomfortable part

The top decile of solo founders earns **61 times the revenue of the median solo founder** in their first six months — and that gap is *widening*, up from 34× four years ago. That's not because the winners are ten times more talented. It's because they make a small set of structural choices — recurring billing, B2B, global from day one — that compound, while everyone else defaults to the path of least resistance.

Pricing is the most under-exercised of those choices, and it's entirely within your control. You don't need a pricing consultant, a cohort analysis, or three months of A/B tests. You need to stop pricing by feel and start pricing by *what you're actually selling*: an outcome someone stops losing sleep over once they pay for it.
