"Solo founder" used to be a phrase investors said with a wince. It read as *couldn't find a co-founder* — a red flag on a term sheet. Going it alone was the backup plan, not the point.

That framing is now dead, and it didn't die by argument. It died the way framings actually die: the data stopped supporting it. This post is about the two datasets that killed it — and about the one number in them that most people quoting these stats get backwards.

## The one-person company is now the default shape

Start with how companies get formed.

**Stripe Atlas: 63% of all new C corps formed through Atlas in Q2 2026 were solo-founded** — an all-time high. That is not a niche. That is the majority shape of new company formation on the platform that files the most of them.

**Carta, measuring a wider population: solo-founded startups went from 23.7% of new companies in 2019 to over a third of all new U.S. companies.** Up 53% in share over six years.

Two datasets, different methodologies, the same direction. The one-person company isn't an aspiration or a thesis anymore. It's the modal outcome.

## The number that gets quoted backwards

Here is where most coverage goes wrong. There's a widely-circulated stat that solo founders are only ~17% of VC-funded deals, and it gets used to argue *"see, solo founders still can't get funded."*

That stat is real (Eqvista, updated 2026-08-18 — solo-founded companies reached 35% of new formations while their share of closed VC rounds stayed flat at 17%). But it does **not** say what people think it says. It doesn't measure whether solo founders *fail to raise*. It measures a **ratio of populations**: lots of new companies form solo, but the companies that go on to raise institutional capital skew multi-founder.

Those are different claims, and the difference matters — because the direct measurement of *how solo founders actually fare when they raise* says something very different.

## The direct measurement: solo founders raise comparable rounds

From the Solo Founders Annual Report, built on Carta's dataset:

```
Series A median valuation
  Solo-founded       $54.9M
  Multi-founder      $53.6M     ← not a gap
```

At Seed and Series B, solo teams land *slightly* lower. At Series A — the round where a company either has a business or doesn't — the medians are **within 2.5% of each other.**

So the 17% figure isn't evidence that solo founders can't raise. It's evidence that the *population of solo-founded companies* is weighted toward companies that were never going to raise — because forming a company alone is now cheap enough that you don't need permission, capital, or a partner to start one. When forming was expensive, everyone who formed was implicitly fundable. When forming is free, the formation pool fills with companies that will bootstrap instead.

**The gate didn't close. More people stopped walking through it.**

## What actually separated the winners

This is where the Stripe data gets genuinely useful, because it measured dispersion rather than averages. Comparing solo-founded Atlas startups incorporated in 2022 and 2023, each with two or more years of revenue:

```
Revenue dispersion, solo founders (first 6 months)
  top decile ÷ median     4 years ago:  34×
  2025:                                  61×
```

The median solo startup's initial six-month revenue was **down 23% year over year**. The top decile was **up 19%**. The gap nearly doubled in four years.

The traits that separated the top decile were not about being solo. They were about what the founder built:

- **AI-native products.** Top-decile solo founders were ~2× as likely to build products whose core functionality depends on AI models. By the two-year mark, AI-native solo startups generated almost 2× the revenue of other solo-founded startups.
- **B2B, not B2C.** Top solo founders were nearly 30% more likely to build for businesses. By month 24, the *median* solo B2B founder out-earned the *median* solo B2C founder by more than 4×. The top decile out-earned by nearly 2×. This held even among bootstrapped companies — so it is not a funding effect.
- **Selling globally from day one.** Top decile sold into **10 countries in month one**; the median sold into 3. By month 24: 40 countries vs 6, and 51% of revenue international vs 2%.
- **Retention early.** ~30% of top-decile customers returned the following month, against 8% at the median.

None of those are solo-specific advantages. They're just what separates a real business from a launched product. Being solo neither conferred them nor prevented them.

## The honest limit, stated plainly

Because the same dataset contains the strongest argument *against* the solo thesis, and skipping it would make this post dishonest:

**By month 24, top-decile multi-founder startups generated 53% more revenue than top-decile solo founders** — and that holds after accounting for investor funding.

So at the top end, teams do pull ahead. The mechanism is unsurprising: two people can cover more surface area, and a company's ceiling is partly set by how much surface one person can hold.

The caveat is where the honest opportunity lives:

```
Bootstrapped startups, 99th percentile of revenue, month 24
  Solo founders came within 5% of multi-founder revenue
```

At the very top of the *bootstrapped* distribution, the gap nearly vanishes. The 53% figure is a statement about the top decile of *funded* companies — the ones built to scale on capital. If you're not raising, the penalty for being alone is dramatically smaller than the headline suggests.

## What this means if you're the one person

Reading the two datasets together produces something more useful than "solo is winning" or "solo can't raise":

1. **Formation cost is no longer the constraint.** 63% of new C corps. Whatever you're building, one person is now a legitimate way to build it. That question is settled.
2. **Funding was never the gate people thought.** Series A medians are near-identical. If your business needs venture capital, being solo is not the obstacle — and if your business doesn't need it, the 17% stat isn't describing you.
3. **The real variable is dispersion, not team size.** Top decile is now 61× the median, up from 34×. The same tools that let one person form a company also let the people who use them well separate from the people who use them as a substitute for judgment.
4. **The mechanisms that separated winners are boring and available to one person:** build something AI-native rather than AI-adjacent, sell to businesses, sell outside your home country from launch, and get retention right before you chase growth.

The one-person company is real now. The interesting question was never whether you're allowed to build one — it was whether what you build is the kind of thing that compounds.
