Every week someone asks the same question in quiet forums and DMs: *"I have a job I don't hate. I want to build my own thing. When do I quit?"*

There's a whole genre of content that answers this with a pep talk. Quit the golden handcuffs. Bet on yourself. The money will follow.

It's not wrong. It's just incomplete — because it only ever shows you the people it worked for.

Here's what leaving *actually* looks like, from people who did it and wrote the receipts down.

## The line isn't "when it's safe" — it's "when the job stops compounding"

The solo founders worth copying didn't quit because they were miserable. They quit because the job had stopped teaching them anything, and the one skill it *would* teach — how to ship something a stranger pays for — was only going to grow outside.

**Arvid Kahl** is the cleanest arc. Two decades in software engineering, a career that looked good on paper. In 2017 his partner, an online English teacher, kept hitting the same repetitive task after every class: writing student feedback. So they built a tiny fix — FeedbackPanda. No team, no investors. Two years later it was doing **$55,000 a month**, and they sold it.

The lesson isn't "side projects turn into exits." It's that the thing he quit *into* was built on a problem he could see from four feet away, not a market thesis.

## Then there's the mirror case

**Michael Lynch** quit Google in 2018 — "Strongly Exceeds Expectations" rating, senior promotion four months out, free-est food in the world. By his own telling, the spark wasn't a grand startup idea; it was the realization that he didn't feel in control of his projects, his career growth, or his direction. So he left, with a plan that mostly amounted to "try different projects for a few months and see if any catch on."

Eight years later, he posts an annual financial recap. His most recent year: **$8,200 profit on $16,300 revenue**. He built TinyPilot, sold it, became a father, and he *still* writes the honest number every single year.

That's the part the pep talks leave out. Leaving the job is not the hard part and it's not the point. It's the *starting line* of an eight-year marathon where the honest outcome can be "modest" as often as "exit."

## So why do it at all?

Because the thing you're really quitting isn't the salary — it's the ceiling. And the data says fewer people are waiting for a "better time" than ever.

Carta's Solo Founders Report puts solo-founded companies at **over one-third of new startups** — roughly double where they sat in 2019. That's not a startup-trend blip. It's the structural fact that AI collapsed the cost of building, so the thing holding most would-be builders back isn't *capability* anymore.

It's permission. And nobody gives you that.

## A decision rule that survives contact with reality

Don't ask "should I quit?" — that's a feeling masquerading as a question. Ask three smaller ones:

1. **Is the job still compounding?** If you're learning nothing new and the only thing growing is your org chart seniority, the salary is a subsidy for stalling.
2. **Is the problem four feet away?** Build from something you can *see* (a repetitive task, a gap in your own work), not a market thesis. Kahl built for a teacher he lived with.
3. **What does "enough" look like in year eight?** If the honest whole-year outcome could be $16K of revenue and you'd still call it worth it, you're ready. If not, the job is still paying you for something real.

The best builders I know didn't leave for a big exit. They left for *control* — of their time, their direction, and whether an idea lives or dies on someone else's roadmap.

You already know which side of that line you're on.
