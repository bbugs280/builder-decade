Most advice about the first hire answers the wrong question.

It tells you *who* to hire — an engineer, a generalist, a contractor first. Or it hands you a feeling: you'll know it's time when you're drowning. Neither is decision-grade. "You'll know" is not a number, and the person who feels most like drowning is often the worst person to be making a hiring call.

The question that actually has data behind it is narrower: **what predicts whether the hire works out?** And the honest answer, from the two best datasets available, is not timing.

## Timing is not the signal

The most useful study here is a First Round analysis of startups in their first two years. It compared companies that made their first non-founder hire within six months of launch against those that waited twelve to eighteen months.

The result is the opposite of what every "move fast" instinct suggests: **timing by itself was not predictive.** Early hirers and late hirers were, on average, equally successful.

That single finding kills most of the advice on this topic. If when you hire doesn't predict the outcome, then every guide that leads with a timeline — "hire before month six," "wait until you have revenue" — is solving for the wrong variable.

What *did* predict it? First Round's answer: **whether you had real revenue or committed pilots at the moment of the hire.** Not MRR of a certain size. Not a headcount feeling. Evidence that the *thing you'd be hiring into* already works.

That's the line worth memorising, because it splits the decision cleanly: **hiring to scale something that already works almost always helps. Hiring to make something start working almost always hurts.**

## The cost side: what a first hire actually is

Before the tactical question, there's the arithmetic. A full-time first hire in a Western market runs roughly **$140–180K fully loaded** in year one — salary, taxes, benefits, equipment, tooling, the recruiter's cut if you use one. That is the number that has to be survivable at your current cash position, not aspirational.

This is where most solo-founder hiring stories go wrong, and the failure is boring rather than dramatic. Hiring extends your burn at exactly the moment your revenue is least proven. A hire you can't fund for **at least six months after the salary is added to your burn** isn't a growth decision — it's a countdown you've started and can't stop.

Run that as arithmetic, not vibes:

```
POST-HIRE RUNWAY  =  current cash ÷ (current monthly burn + new salary)
Safe to proceed when that number ≥ 6 months.
```

It's not a magic revenue threshold. Two founders at the same MRR can be on opposite sides of it, because what matters is the runway *after* the hire, not the number on the dashboard before it.

## The data most solo founders ignore

Here's where the two datasets combine into something genuinely useful — and genuinely under-discussed.

Carta's solo-founder research found three things that sit awkwardly next to each other:

1. **Solo founders hire *faster* than multi-founder companies.** Median time from incorporation to first hire: **399 days for solo founders** versus **480 days for multi-founder teams.** Nearly three months sooner.
2. **Solo founders hold roughly double the equity to work with.** A two-person founding team has already split the company 50/50 before hiring anyone. A solo founder still owns 100% — an enormous war chest for attracting a first key hire.
3. **And they don't use it.** Median equity granted to the **first hire is essentially identical** across solo- and multi-founder companies — about **1.5%** either way. The fifth hire lands around 0.30% in both.

Read those three together and the conclusion is hard to miss. **The advantage exists, is real, and is being left on the table.** A solo founder's structural edge in hiring isn't speed and isn't capital — it's *equity headroom*, and the data says they hand out the same package a co-founded startup does, as if they had the same split to absorb.

Carta's own commentary puts it plainly: for a first-hire grant made in 2023–2024, the number clustered at roughly 1.5% *regardless of founding structure*. Not a little under-used. Barely differentiated at all.

⚠️ **The honest caveat, stated up front:** about **1.5%** is a *median across companies that hired*, most of which were venture-backed and expected to raise more. If you're building a deliberately bootstrapped one-person company that will never have a cap table, equity conversation isn't your lever at all — cash and runway is. The finding is a real signal about a real gap; it is not a prescription to start handing out equity you never intended to issue.

## So what actually predicts a good first hire

Put the evidence together and the decision collapses to three checks, in order:

**1. Is there a working thing to scale?** Not effort. Not a plan. Evidence that the thing you'd be hiring into already produces — a paying customer, a committed pilot, a retention curve that holds. This is First Round's finding, and it's the one that actually predicts.

**2. Can you survive the hire?** Six months of runway *after* the new salary hits the burn. Below that, the hire converts your runway problem into a payroll problem, which is a strictly worse version of the same problem.

**3. Are you paying with the right currency?** This is the under-used one. If you're venture-track and solo, you hold roughly twice the equity headroom of a co-founded competitor at the same stage — and the market data says you're probably not deploying it. If you're deliberately bootstrapped, the reverse: your currency is cash and your constraint is runway, and a 1.5% equity grant is noise.

**The honest summary:** timing isn't the signal, a working thing is, and most solo founders are sitting on the one advantage the data says they ignore.

That's a narrower answer than "you'll know when you're drowning." It's also one you can act on.

If you're earlier than this — not ready for a full-time hire but with more work than hours — the [contractor-versus-employee trade](/posts/when-to-hire-your-first-contractor/) is the decision you actually have in front of you, and it's a different calculation. And if you're still deciding whether the one-person shape holds at all, start with [why the one-person company is no longer the exception](/posts/one-person-company-is-real-now/).

- **Carta — "Startups Are Waiting Longer to Make Their First Hire" (Peter Walker, 2025-05-19).** — Median days from incorporation to first hire rose from **214 days in 2019 to 284 days in 2024** (+32%); the same pattern holds for the 2nd and 3rd hires. Attributed to founders being more capital-efficient and leveraging AI tooling to stay leaner.
- **Carta — *Founder Ownership Report 2025* (Peter Walker, Kevin Dowd; 45,000+ startups incorporated 2015–2024).** — Solo-founder share of new incorporations reached ~35% in 2024 (more than doubling over a decade); solo founders comprised 35% of incorporations but only 17% of companies closing a VC round. Context for the funding asymmetry alongside the equity-headroom finding.
- **Cost-of-hire figure** — a $140–180K fully-loaded first-year cost is a widely used planning range in US/Western markets and varies materially by role and geography. Treat it as an order-of-magnitude planning input, not a measured constant.
