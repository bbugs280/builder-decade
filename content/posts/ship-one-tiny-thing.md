Everyone wants the breakthrough — the big launch, the viral hit, the overnight success. And everyone quietly knows that's not how it works. What actually works is so boring it gets ignored: **ship one tiny thing, every single week, without fail.**

The system doesn't sound impressive. That's the point. It's the unglamorous, compounding engine that separates people who *have* a body of work from people who *want* one. Here's the whole thing.

## Why "tiny" is the whole trick

The instinct is to ship big — the polished app, the complete product, the thing that will impress people. But big things have three problems a tiny thing never has:

- **Big things take long enough that the novelty dies before you finish** (see the week-three wall). Tiny things land before the trough hits.
- **Big things are rarely "done."** There's always more to polish, so you never actually ship. A tiny thing has a clear end — you can hold the win condition in one sentence.
- **Big things are scary to start.** Tiny things are too small to be afraid of, so you start them, and starting is the whole battle.

The tiny thing isn't a stepping stone to the big thing. The tiny thing *is* the system. The big thing is just what a thousand tiny things look like from the outside.

## The exact loop

The system has four moves, and none of them are optional:

1. **Pick a thing small enough to finish in a week.** Not "build an app." Something with a one-sentence win condition: "a script that renames my screenshots," "a single landing page," "one working API call."
2. **Ship it, ugly.** The bar is *done*, not *good*. If it works, it ships. Polish is for later, and "later" is a trap.
3. **Show one person.** The accountability is the point. One person who sees it and asks about the next one.
4. **Write down what you learned.** One line, in a running log. The log is the compound interest — a year of logs is a personal playbook no course can sell you.

That's it. Four moves, one week, repeat. No dashboard, no streak app, no optimization.

## Why it compounds (the honest math)

Here's the part that looks like magic afterward but is just accumulation:

- **Skill compounds.** Every tiny thing teaches you something the last one didn't. Fifty tiny things later, you've hit and solved fifty real problems — which is more than most people hit in a career of *planning* to build.
- **Confidence compounds.** Each shipped thing, however ugly, is evidence that you can ship. That evidence is what makes the next one easier to start, not harder.
- **Reputation compounds.** A person with fifty shipped things — even small ones — is a *builder*. It's a fact about you that no credential can fake. A body of work is the strongest resume there is.
- **Luck compounds.** Shipped things are lottery tickets that are actually out in the world. A script you shipped might be seen by the right person. An unpublished plan can't be discovered by anyone.

The tiny things don't just add up. They *multiply*, because each one makes every future one easier.

## What a year looks like

Week one: a script. Month three: a small tool you actually use. Month six: something with a user who isn't you. Month twelve: a body of work, a real skill, and a backlog of "I could build that" confidence.

From the outside, it'll look like you "got good" — like talent arrived. From the inside, you'll know it was just fifty tiny things, one per week, that no one watched you ship.

That's the entire system. It's not a shortcut. It's the only reliable path there is — and it's available to anyone willing to ship things that are slightly embarrassing, every week, until they stop being embarrassing.
