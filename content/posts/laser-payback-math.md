Search "does a laser engraver pay for itself" and the internet hands you a number. Usually under two months. That number is arrived at with a real formula, and the formula is correct. What gets slipped past you is the sales volume plugged into it.

So here is the whole sum in the open: the vendor's version, the same arithmetic with a real sales volume, and the three lines you should add before you spend the money.

## The claim, verbatim

xTool's own buyer guide for Etsy sellers, published May 2026, works through a personalised engraved cutting board. I am quoting it directly so you can check me.

- Selling price: €42.00
- Variable cost per order: €15.50 (board €9.50, packaging €1.20, Etsy and payment fees €4.80)
- Gross profit per unit: €26.50
- Machine: €3,399
- **At 35 boards a week: payback 0.85 months**
- **At 15 boards a week: payback 1.97 months**

Run their own numbers and they are internally consistent. €26.50 × 35 × 4.33 = €4,016 a month, and €3,399 ÷ €4,016 = 0.85. Nobody has to cheat to get there.

The reason to read it slowly anyway is who wrote it. That page sits on the site of the company selling the €3,399 machine, in a section called "Buyer Guide". The formula is sound. The person publishing it earns money when you buy the machine, and earns nothing whether your boards sell or not.

## The number they leave for you to find

The vendor does not hide the weakness. It is in the same document. The part that carries no evidence at all is the "35 boards a week".

Etsy's 2025 annual report gives the figures you need to test it:

- Etsy marketplace gross merchandise sales, 2025: **$10,460.7 million**
- Etsy marketplace active sellers, 31 Dec 2025: **5.6 million**

Divide one by the other. That is **$1,868 a year per active seller**, or about **$156 a month of sales** across the whole shop.

Not profit. Sales, before a single fee, before materials, before the machine.

The 35 boards in the vendor's example is €4,016 of gross profit a month. Etsy's average shop takes $156 a month in *revenue*. The example describes a shop roughly **26 times** the average.

And "average seller" is already the flattering measure. Etsy's own seller survey found 70% of sellers consider their shop a business and 29% say creative work is their sole occupation. The other 70% are not running 35 boards a week. The 29% are not all running it either.

## The same sum with a normal shop's volume

Keep everything else identical: same €42 board, same €26.50 margin, same €3,399 machine. Change only how many you sell, to the point where the shop's total monthly sales match Etsy's average of $156.

That works out to fewer than **two boards a week**. Payback on the machine becomes **21.8 months**.

And that 21.8 months is the *generous* version, because it treats the whole $156 as profit. It is not profit. It is sales, before Etsy's 6.5% transaction fee, before payment processing (3% plus $0.25 in the US), before board cost, before packaging, before shipping.

Here is the ladder, all with the same €26.50 per-unit margin:

| Boards per week | Gross profit a month | Payback on a €3,399 machine |
|---|---|---|
| 35 | €4,016 | 0.85 months |
| 15 | €1,721 | 1.97 months |
| 5 | €574 | 5.9 months |
| 2 | €229 | 14.8 months |
| Under 1 | €115 | 29.6 months |

The vendor published the top two rows. Every row uses their formula.

## Volume is the only thing that moves, and it is the only thing you cannot buy

Read the table again and notice what is identical in every row. The machine price never changes. The margin per board never changes. The only variable is how many people hand you money each week, and that is the one input the machine cannot supply.

Which reframes what you are buying. A laser is not a business with a payback period. It is a **capacity purchase**. It removes a bottleneck you already have. If you have no orders, the bottleneck is not cutting speed, and no machine on earth will fix it.

That leads to the only two honest reasons to buy one:

**You already have the orders.** You are turning work away, or paying someone else to cut it, or outsourcing with a two-week lead time that loses you sales. Then the payback sum is real, because the volume is already in your books. This is the case the vendor's formula quietly assumes and never states.

**You are buying it as a tool, not an investment.** You want to make things, and €3,399 for a machine you enjoy using is a decision about your hobby budget, not a business case. That is a completely reasonable purchase. Just do not tell yourself it is a 0.85-month payback, because it is not, and you will not be able to keep believing it in month four.

## Three lines the vendor's formula leaves out

**One: time.** 35 finished boards a week is seven boards a day with no day off. The formula counts materials and platform fees, and it does not count the hours of sanding, masking, or the customer messages asking whether the name can be spelled differently. Your hour does not appear in that number. There is no version of this where you sell 35 bespoke items a week and it costs you nothing.

**Two: everything after the sticker price.** An extractor fan and filtration, a honeycomb bed, blanks and material, spare lenses, the software upgrade that turns out to be needed. The "€3,399" is the entry point, not the total. Whatever that adds, add it to the top of the fraction.

**Three: what you keep doing when the orders are thin.** The machine does not only pay back on success. It sits there in a slow month and costs you nothing extra, no salary, no rent. That is genuinely in its favour and it is why a machine is a lower-risk purchase than hiring a person. Say it honestly, on both sides.

## What to do with all this

Do not price the decision off a payback period someone else computed. Price it off two questions you can answer about your own shop this week:

**How many can you actually sell in a month?** Not what you could make. What sold in the last 30 days, plus what is already on order. Use the real number. If you have never sold any, then your first sale does not need a laser, and you can test the product by hand or through a print-on-demand service for a fraction of the cost.

**What is the machine actually replacing?** If it replaces an outsourced cutter with a two-week turnaround, the value is real and measurable. If it replaces nothing at all, you are buying capacity ahead of demand, and that is a bet on you selling, not a calculation.

Then take your number, not theirs, and divide.

The formula was never the problem. The 35 boards a week was, and only you know your own figure.
