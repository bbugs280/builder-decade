For most of startup history, "who's your co-founder?" wasn't really a question. It was the first box on a checklist, right after "what's your idea?" Go it alone and the first thing you'd hear was *you'll wish you had a team* — as if a co-founder were insurance you'd bought, not a person you'd now be legally and emotionally tied to for a decade.

The AI era hasn't made co-founders obsolete. It has made the *default* obsolete. The honest question stopped being "how do I find a co-founder?" and became "do I actually need one — and what am I trading away to get one?" This post is a receipt-backed decision rule for answering that question.

## Why "just get a co-founder" was a myth all along

The default-to-team advice rests on one assumption: that a team is inherently safer. The data doesn't back it. Per Carta's Solo Founders Report, solo founders **hire their first employee at a median of 399 days** — faster than multi-founder teams, who wait a median of **480 days**. The intuition flips: solo founders don't sit around missing a team. They reach delegating size *sooner*.

The equity story is starker. A solo founder retains a materially larger stake through the whole lifecycle — Carta's data shows the median solo founder holds **75% more ownership at exit** than the lead founder of a multi-founder company. By Series A, a multi-founder *team's* combined ownership falls to a median of 36%, split three or four ways, with individual founders often under 15%. You are not buying safety with a co-founder. You are, at the point that matters most, buying dilution.

## The one place the market still punishes solo founders — and what it actually means

There *is* a real, citable penalty. In 2024, solo-founded companies were 30% of new startups but raised only **14.7%** of priced-equity dollars. Venture capital still structurally prefers teams.

This is the moment to be clear-eyed instead of defensive. The bias is real, but it is not evidence that solo is weaker — it is evidence that the *financing layer* lags the *building layer*. Carta runs on tens of thousands of companies and still reports the solo share rising every single year. The penalty is a fundraising friction, not a building penalty. If your plan requires venture scale on a venture timeline, a co-founder may buy you credibility you can't build fast enough. If it doesn't, you're paying for insurance you never file a claim on.

## The decision rule

Forget "team vs solo" as a moral question. When you're actually deciding, only four things genuinely matter:

1. **The capital gate.** Does your path require venture money in the next 18 months? If yes, a co-founder can be rational — not because you need the *work*, but because the financing layer still rewards teams. If you're bootstrapping or building to revenue, the 14.7% bias barely touches you.
2. **The skill gap — build vs. buy.** Is the missing capability a *one-time* gap (you need a design pass, a logo, a legal review) or an *ongoing* one (you can't code at all and the product is code)? One-time gaps are contractors, not co-founders. In the AI era, even many ongoing gaps got cheaper to buy than to split equity for.
3. **The psychology — do you ship alone or stall alone?** Some people build faster with a witness; their own accountability needs an audience. Others are slowed by alignment overhead. Be honest about which you are. A co-founder who fixes your motivation is worth the dilution; one who adds *to* your process cost is not.
4. **The wrong-co-founder test.** The real regret stat isn't "solo founders fail." It's the number of founders who spent the decisive year entangled with the wrong person. A good co-founder outperforms solo. A bad one is worse than solo by a wide margin — and good ones are rare and slow to find. The cost of a *wrong* co-founder is why "default to team" is the dangerous advice, not "default to solo."

## The updated default

The correct default in 2026 has quietly inverted. It is not "find a co-founder first." It is: **start solo, and add a co-founder only when a specific, named reason crosses the bar** — a capital gate you can't clear alone, or a skill gap too strategic to outsource, or a relationship so proven it lowers your risk rather than adding to your coordination load.

What changed is that the boring prerequisites are gone. You no longer need a co-founder to *build*. The only remaining reasons to take one are reasons you can actually name — and if you can't name one, you have your answer. That's the honest upside of the AI era: not that solo is always right, but that it's now a real choice instead of a compromise.
